Then the two results are combined for final triage.  2. Why do we need two modules? FLAIR and DWI/ADC answer different clinical questions. FLAIR FLAIR is useful for detecting many general abnormalities such as: • white-matter abnormalities  • edema  • tumors  • infarcts  • demyelination  • structural abnormalities  • some infections/inflammatory changes  So it is useful for broad screening: "Does this brain MRI look generally normal or abnormal?" DWI + ADC These are particularly important for acute ischemic stroke. A typical acute infarct may show: DWI  → high signal ADC  → corresponding low signal So the stroke module asks: "Is there evidence of an acute ischemic stroke?" That is why stroke gets its own dedicated module instead of being treated as just another FLAIR abnormality.  3. 13. Absolutely critical rule: patient-level splitting Never split like this: Patient A Slice 1 → Train Patient A Slice 2 → Train Patient A Slice 20 → Test   
❌ That creates leakage. MRI slices belonging to the same patient are highly similar. The model may therefore appear to perform extremely well simply because it has already seen another slice from that patient. Correct: Patient A → TRAIN ONLY Patient B → TRAIN ONLY Patient C → VALIDATION ONLY Patient D → TEST ONLY All slices follow their patient.  14. What is DINOv2 doing? DINOv2 is a self-supervised Vision Transformer foundation model. It was pretrained on a huge collection of ordinary images without requiring conventional manual class labels. It learned useful visual patterns such as: shapes edges textures structures spatial relationships semantic patterns We reuse those representations for MRI. Important: DINOv2 is primarily a 2D image encoder. 
--- Page 11 ---
It does not directly take our complete 25-slice MRI study. Instead: Slice 1 → DINOv2 → Feature vector Slice 2 → DINOv2 → Feature vector Slice 3 → DINOv2 → Feature vector ... For DINOv2-Base, one possible global representation is approximately: 768768  features per slice.  15. What is a feature vector? Instead of retaining the entire raw image, DINOv2 converts the image into numbers describing its visual characteristics. For example: MRI slice    ↓ DINOv2    ↓ [0.32, -0.81, 1.12, 0.04, ...]           768 values You shouldn't interpret one number as: feature 327 = tumor. It is a learned high-dimensional representation.  16. Frozen DINOv2 vs fine-tuning Initially we plan: DINOv2 = frozen Meaning its original weights are not changed. We train only the small new layers that we add. Why? 
--- Page 12 ---
Because our hospital dataset may not be huge. Freezing gives: • lower training cost  • less overfitting  • faster experiments  • easier debugging  Then an ablation can compare: Frozen DINOv2 vs Last few blocks fine-tuned We should let validation performance decide whether fine-tuning helps.  17. Module 1 architecture The proposed Module 1 is: Coronal FLAIR DICOM         │         ▼ Preprocessing / QC         │         ▼ Relevant ordered slices         │         ▼ DINOv2 applied to each slice         │         ▼ Slice embeddings         │         + Relative slice position         │         ▼ Position-aware attention pooling         │         ▼ One patient embedding         │         ▼ MLP classifier         │         ▼ Normal / Abnormal  18. What is slice-level analysis? 
--- Page 13 ---
Suppose Patient A has 25 slices. A purely slice-level system would do: Slice 1 → Normal Slice 2 → Normal Slice 3 → Abnormal ... Slice 25 → Normal But then: What is the prediction for the patient? That's the problem. In the real world the radiologist is interested in: Patient A's examination → Normal or Abnormal not 25 unrelated predictions.  19. What is patient-level analysis? Patient-level analysis means: ALL relevant slices         ↓ combined intelligently         ↓ one representation         ↓ ONE final patient prediction So: 25 slices    ↓ Patient A    ↓ Abnormal That is what our attention pooling does.  20. Why not simply average all slices? The simplest method is: 
--- Page 14 ---
Mean pooling Suppose DINO generates: z1 z2 z3 ... z25 Mean pooling calculates: zpatient=1N∑iziz_{patient} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i z_i  This gives every slice equal importance. Problem: Maybe only 2 out of 25 slices show a small lesion. Mean pooling treats those 2 slices exactly like the 23 less informative slices.  21. What about majority voting? Another method is: Slice 1 → Normal Slice 2 → Normal Slice 3 → Abnormal ... Then count: Normal = 20 Abnormal = 5 Patient prediction: Normal This is majority voting. Problem: What if those 5 abnormal slices contain a genuine lesion? A minority of slices can still contain the clinically critical information. 
--- Page 15 ---
Majority voting also throws away the rich DINO feature vectors and retains only: 0 / 1  22. What is max pooling? Another possibility is to take the strongest activation across slices. But this can make the decision depend too heavily on one extreme slice and does not learn which patterns are useful across the study.  23. Attention pooling Attention pooling learns: "Which slices should contribute more strongly to this patient's final prediction?" Suppose there are five slices. The network could learn: Slice Attention 1 0.03 2 0.07 3 0.60 4 0.25 5 0.05 Then: zpatient=0.03z1+0.07z2+0.60z3+0.25z4+0.05z5z_{patient} = 0.03z_1 + 0.07z_2 + 0.60z_3 + 0.25z_4 + 0.05z_5  The weights are learned automatically. So: Mean pooling → all slices equally important  Majority voting → all slice decisions equally important  Attention pooling → importance is learned from the data 
--- Page 16 ---
This is one of the most important concepts in our architecture.  24. What exactly is position-aware attention? Ordinary attention receives: slice features But MRI slices form an ordered anatomical stack. Slice 3 and Slice 23 do not come from the same approximate position. So for each slice we calculate a relative position. If the study has NN slices: pi=iN−1p_i=\frac{i}{N-1}  This converts positions roughly into: 0 ───────── 0.5 ───────── 1 start       middle         end For a 21-slice patient: middle ≈ 0.5 For a 25-slice patient: middle ≈ 0.5 So it works even when patients have different slice counts. Then the attention mechanism gets: DINOv2 feature + relative slice position and learns: what does the slice contain AND where approximately is it located within this MRI stack?  
--- Page 17 ---
25. Why is position-aware attention useful? Without position: DINOv2 feature → what does this image look like? With position: DINOv2 feature → what does it look like? Position       → where in the stack was it? So the MRI is no longer treated as just an unordered collection of pictures.  26. What happens if patients have different slice counts? This is completely manageable. Patient A: 25 slices → 25 DINO vectors → attention → one 768-D patient vector Patient B: 21 slices → 21 DINO vectors → attention → one 768-D patient vector Patient C: 29 slices → 29 DINO vectors → attention → one 768-D patient vector The output size is always fixed. During batch training, we can pad shorter sequences: Patient A = 25 real slices Patient B = 21 real + 4 padded and provide a mask telling attention: 
--- Page 18 ---
Ignore padded positions.  27. How exactly do DWI and ADC get fused? Suppose DINOv2-Base produces: DWIi→768−DDWI_i \rightarrow 768-D  and: ADCi→768−DADC_i \rightarrow 768-D  
--- Page 19 ---
We concatenate them: [768+768]=1536−D[768 + 768] = 1536-D  Then use a small MLP: 1536→7681536 \rightarrow 768  So: DWI feature ─┐              ├→ concatenate → MLP → fused feature ADC feature ─┘ This is called: Feature-level fusion It lets both sequences remain distinct until meaningful representations have been extracted.  29. Why not just make DWI and ADC RGB channels? Because DWI and ADC are not simply different colors. They are different MRI measurements with different physical intensity meanings. So we don't want: R = DWI G = ADC B = something as the main approach. Instead: DWI → feature extraction ADC → feature extraction then fuse features which is methodologically cleaner.  30. Major research gaps we are addressing 
--- Page 20 ---
The literature shows that many pieces already exist independently. But there are several gaps. Gap: slice-level classification Some papers classify individual MRI slices. Our response: patient-level attention pooling across the entire relevant MRI study.  Gap: manually selected abnormal slices Some stroke work gives the model only obvious lesion-containing slices. Our response: analyse the full relevant study and let attention identify useful slices.  Gap: majority voting / simple averaging These approaches either: • give every slice equal weight  • or count binary predictions.  Our response: learn the contribution of every slice using attention.  Gap: lack of explicit slice position 2D slice encoders do not inherently know where each slice occurred in the volume. Our response: relative positional information + attention.  Gap: fixed-volume CNN input Some 3D CNN papers convert every MRI to a fixed number of slices such as 16. This can require: 
--- Page 21 ---
• interpolation  • downsampling  • discarding information.  Our response: variable-length slice processing with DINOv2 + attention pooling.  Gap: limited DWI–ADC integration Some stroke systems use DWI only. Others use DWI/ADC but still perform simple slice-level classification. Our response: explicit paired DWI–ADC feature fusion before patient-level aggregation.  Gap: conventional CNNs dominate Many approaches train CNNs/3D CNNs from scratch or heavily fine-tune them. Our response: explore pretrained self-supervised DINOv2 representations, especially useful for a smaller hospital dataset.  Gap: tasks are usually isolated Literature often does: abnormality classification only OR stroke only OR tumor classification only Our response: General abnormality branch + dedicated acute-stroke branch + clinical triage fusion  31. 39. The simplest way to explain the whole idea to your teammates You can tell them: “We are building two patient-level MRI classifiers.                                             Question:  i just have a doubt regarding our project.. so each patient may have different number of slices like one may have 25, other may have 21 and so on... how do we handle that or is dinov2 capable of handling that ? ANSWER:

Yes — this is actually one of the reasons our proposed DINOv2 + attention-pooling approach fits your project well. The important distinction is: DINOv2 itself does not process the whole 21-slice or 25-slice MRI study at once. It processes each 2D slice individually. So Patient A having 25 slices and Patient B having 21 slices is completely manageable. Example For Patient A with 25 FLAIR slices: 25 slices → DINOv2 applied 25 times → 25 feature vectors For Patient B with 21 slices: 21 slices → DINOv2 applied 21 times → 21 feature vectors If we're using DINOv2-B and a 768-D representation: PatientA→25×768Patient_A \rightarrow 25 \times 768 PatientB→21×768Patient_B \rightarrow 21 \times 768  DINOv2 does not care that the patients have different numbers of slices, because it only sees one resized 2D image at a time. Then attention pooling handles the variable number of slices For Patient A: z1,z2,…,z25z_1,z_2,\ldots,z_{25}  For Patient B: z1,z2,…,z21z_1,z_2,\ldots,z_{21}  The attention layer calculates weights over whatever number of slices are available. For Patient A: α1+α2+⋯+α25=1\alpha_1+\alpha_2+\cdots+\alpha_{25}=1  For Patient B: α1+α2+⋯+α21=1\alpha_1+\alpha_2+\cdots+\alpha_{21}=1  Then: 
--- Page 29 ---
zpatient=∑iαiziz_{patient}=\sum_i \alpha_i z_i  Regardless of whether there were 21, 25, or 30 slices, the final output is always one fixed-size patient embedding, for example: 768−D768-D  That 768-D patient embedding can then go into the classifier.  What about position if patients have different slice counts? This is exactly why we use relative position, not just absolute slice number. For each patient: pi=iN−1p_i=\frac{i}{N-1}  So if one patient has 21 slices and another has 25, their positions are still normalized roughly between: 0→10 \rightarrow 1  For example: Location 21-slice patient 25-slice patient Beginning 0 0 Middle ~0.5 ~0.5 End 1 1 Therefore the model understands: “this slice is approximately in the middle of this patient's stack” rather than depending on something arbitrary like slice number 11.  So we do NOT need to force every patient to 16 or 25 slices That's actually an advantage compared with a fixed-volume approach such as the Gauriau paper. We don't need: 25 slices → artificially reduce to 16 
--- Page 30 ---
or 21 slices → duplicate/interpolate until 25. Instead: Use the relevant available slices → DINOv2 → position-aware attention pooling. We would still perform basic slice filtering, for example removing extreme slices with almost no brain tissue, but we don't need every patient to end up with exactly the same number.  One small technical detail during training Neural networks usually train multiple patients in one batch, and tensors like equal dimensions. Suppose one batch contains: • Patient 1 → 21 slices  • Patient 2 → 25 slices  • Patient 3 → 23 slices  We can temporarily pad them to the maximum: • Patient 1 → 21 real + 4 padded  • Patient 2 → 25 real  • Patient 3 → 23 real + 2 padded  Then we use an attention mask telling the network: “Ignore these padded positions.” So padded slices receive no attention and do not influence the prediction. Conceptually: Variable number of real slices → padding only for batching → mask padded values → attention over real slices only.  So the complete idea is Patient with N slices 
--- Page 31 ---
→ preprocess each slice → DINOv2 on every slice → NN feature vectors → add relative position → position-aware attention pooling over N slices → one fixed-size patient representation → Normal / Abnormal And the exact same principle works for Module 2, except the units being aggregated are paired DWI–ADC slice features. So yes: different slice counts are not a problem for our architecture. In fact, variable-length patient studies are one of the reasons we're choosing slice-level DINOv2 features + attention pooling rather than forcing everyone into a fixed 3D volume.
