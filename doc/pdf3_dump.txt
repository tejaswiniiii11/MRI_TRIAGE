Project title Multi-Sequence AI-Based Brain MRI Triage System Using Vision Foundation Models for General Abnormality Classification and Acute Ischemic Stroke Detection The aim is not to automatically diagnose every neurological disease. The aim is to triage brain MRI examinations before the radiologist reviews them, so potentially important studies can be moved higher in the worklist. The final system has two independently trained modules:                          BRAIN MRI STUDY                                │               ┌────────────────┴────────────────┐               │                                 │         CORONAL T2-FLAIR                    DWI + ADC               │                                 │           MODULE 1                          MODULE 2               │                                 │      General abnormality               Acute stroke detection               │                                 │        Normal / Abnormal                Stroke / No Stroke               │                                 │               └────────────────┬────────────────┘                                │                         Decision Fusion                                │                     Routine / Urgent / Immediate For now, Module 1 can be trained as Normal vs Abnormal. Borderline cases can be collected separately and introduced later as a third class once there are enough examples.  1. what a brain MRI actually looks like An MRI examination is not one image. A particular MRI sequence consists of a stack of 2D slices through the brain. For example, a coronal FLAIR study might contain: Patient A  Slice 01 Slice 02 Slice 03 ... Slice 25 while another patient might have only: 
--- Page 2 ---
Patient B  Slice 01 ... Slice 21 This variation is normal. The complete ordered stack represents the brain volume. There are three common viewing planes: AXIAL Horizontal slices looking from bottom → top  CORONAL Front-to-back slices like looking at the patient from the front  SAGITTAL Side-to-side slices For our project: Module 1 uses coronal T2-FLAIR because that is the routine sequence available in the hospital dataset. Module 2 will generally use axial DWI and ADC for acute ischemic stroke.  2. What is FLAIR? FLAIR stands for: Fluid-Attenuated Inversion Recovery. It suppresses the bright signal from cerebrospinal fluid and makes many brain abnormalities easier to see. It can reveal findings such as white-matter lesions, edema, infarcts, demyelination, tumors and various structural abnormalities. Therefore Module 1 asks: Does the patient's FLAIR study contain a convincing abnormality? It initially outputs: Normal or 
--- Page 3 ---
Abnormal It is intentionally a broad screening task rather than trying to identify every possible disease.  3. What are DWI and ADC? DWI stands for: Diffusion-Weighted Imaging. For acute ischemic stroke, a commonly used diffusion image is b1000, meaning: b=1000 s/mm2b = 1000\ \text{s/mm}^2  A fresh infarct commonly appears: DWI b1000 → bright ADC       → low / dark ADC stands for: Apparent Diffusion Coefficient. The ADC map helps confirm that the DWI brightness represents true restricted diffusion rather than another phenomenon. Therefore our stroke module uses: DWI + ADC      ↓ Stroke / No Stroke The b1000 value is an MRI acquisition parameter associated with the DWI series, not a class label in the Excel sheet.  4. What is DICOM? Hospital MRI images are normally stored in DICOM format. A DICOM file contains: Image pixels + medical/image-acquisition metadata

Metadata can contain information such as: Patient ID Study ID Series Description Image Position Image Orientation Pixel Spacing Slice Thickness Scanner manufacturer etc. Therefore the original hospital dataset should remain in DICOM until it has been safely de-identified.  5. What is SynthStrip? SynthStrip is a tool used for robust brain extraction/skull stripping. It produces a brain mask. Conceptually: MRI  ↓ SynthStrip  ↓ Brain mask  
--- Page 8 ---
0 = outside brain 1 = brain Important implementation detail: I would not necessarily replace the original MRI with a skull-stripped image. Instead, use the mask primarily to determine: Where is the brain? Then calculate a bounding box around the mask and crop the original MRI intensities with a safety margin. So: Original MRI       + SynthStrip mask       ↓ Find brain bounding box       ↓ Expand box slightly       ↓ Crop original MRI This removes large amounts of useless black background while avoiding an excessively tight crop.  12. What about the first and last slices? The beginning and end of many MRI stacks may contain: • very little brain tissue,  • partial anatomy,  • edge effects,  • low useful information.  However, we should not blindly delete exactly the first 3 and last 3 slices for every patient. Different patients have different: • slice counts,  • anatomical coverage,  • positioning.  A better solution is to use the SynthStrip brain mask. 
--- Page 9 ---
For every slice calculate something like: brain coverage=brain-mask pixelstotal pixels\text{brain coverage} = \frac{\text{brain-mask pixels}} {\text{total pixels}}  Then exclude slices where brain coverage falls below a chosen threshold. Conceptually: Slice 1 → 1% brain   → remove Slice 2 → 4% brain   → remove Slice 3 → 18% brain  → keep ... Slice 22 → 16% brain → keep Slice 23 → 3% brain  → remove The threshold should be chosen from data/QC rather than invented blindly. So the idea is: remove edge slices based on actual brain coverage, not fixed slice numbers.  13. Slight patient tilt Some patients may lie slightly tilted in the scanner. We should not automatically discard every mildly tilted scan. DINOv2 and augmentation can tolerate modest variation. Grossly abnormal orientation or severe acquisition problems should be flagged during QC. We should avoid aggressive geometric correction unless genuinely necessary because unnecessary resampling can alter the image.  14. Intensity preprocessing MRI intensity values do not have the same fixed physical scale as CT. One scanner/patient may produce an image that looks darker or brighter than another. Therefore preprocessing should normalize intensity. A reasonable Module 1 strategy is: 
--- Page 10 ---
Use pixels within brain mask        ↓ Robust percentile clipping        ↓ Brain-based z-score normalization For example, extreme values can be clipped using percentiles before normalization. N4 bias-field correction may be tested later as an ablation, rather than automatically making it mandatory.  15. Resize without distorting anatomy Do not stretch every MRI directly into a square. Instead: Original crop e.g. 210 × 180       ↓ Resize longest dimension       ↓ preserve aspect ratio       ↓ pad remaining area       ↓ 224 × 224 This avoids changing anatomical proportions. We can initially use 224 × 224, since that works naturally with DINOv2-B/14, and perhaps later test 336 resolution if computationally feasible.  16. Why three channels if MRI is grayscale? DINOv2 expects a 3-channel input because it was trained on ordinary RGB images. MRI is grayscale. We therefore use: X→[X,X,X] so: Channel R = MRI Channel G = MRI 
--- Page 11 ---
Channel B = MRI No fake colors are added.  17. What exactly is DINOv2? DINOv2 is a self-supervised vision foundation model based on a Vision Transformer. It was originally trained on a huge collection of ordinary images. It has learned general visual representations involving things like: • shapes,  • edges,  • textures,  • structures,  • relationships between image regions.  We are not asking DINOv2 to diagnose an MRI directly. We use it as a feature extractor. For one MRI slice: MRI slice    ↓ DINOv2-B/14    ↓ feature vector If we use the CLS representation from DINOv2-Base: slice→768-dimensional vector So: Slice 1 → [768 values] Slice 2 → [768 values] Slice 3 → [768 values] ... Every slice gets the same feature-vector dimension.  18. Very important: we are NOT doing slice-level classification 
--- Page 12 ---
We are doing: slice-level feature extraction → patient-level classification Not: Slice 1 → Normal Slice 2 → Normal Slice 3 → Abnormal Instead: Slice 1 → 768-D feature Slice 2 → 768-D feature Slice 3 → 768-D feature ...               ↓         Aggregate features               ↓       one patient prediction This distinction is important.  19. Different patients have different numbers of slices This architecture naturally supports that. Patient A: 25×76825\times768  Patient B: 21×76821\times768  Patient C: 28×76828\times768  The number of feature vectors varies. The size of each feature vector remains fixed. Then attention pooling converts the variable-length sequence into one fixed patient vector.  
--- Page 13 ---
20. Why not mean pooling? Mean pooling would simply calculate: zpatient=1N∑i=1Nziz_{patient} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} z_i  Every slice gets equal importance. That may not be ideal because an abnormality might exist on only a few slices.  21. Why not majority voting? Majority voting first makes a slice prediction: Slice 1 → Normal Slice 2 → Normal Slice 3 → Abnormal ... and then counts them. The problem is that a small lesion may appear on only a few slices. If: 20 slices = Normal 5 slices  = Abnormal majority voting could potentially classify the whole study as normal. It also reduces a rich 768-D representation to a binary label too early.  22. Attention pooling (The aggregation method to be used )  Attention pooling instead learns: Which slice features matter most for the patient-level prediction? Suppose: Slice 1 → 0.02 Slice 2 → 0.05 
--- Page 14 ---
Slice 3 → 0.55 Slice 4 → 0.30 Slice 5 → 0.08 These weights sum approximately to 1. Then: zpatient=∑iαiziz_{patient} = \sum_i \alpha_i z_i  The model therefore learns to give more importance to informative slices.  23. Why position-aware attention? Ordinary slice attention knows: what does the slice contain? But MRI is an ordered anatomical stack. We also want: where approximately was this slice located in the stack? So for slice ii among NN slices: Pi = i/N-1 This maps position to approximately: 0 ---------------- 0.5 ---------------- 1 beginning          middle               end This works even if: Patient A = 21 slices Patient B = 25 slices because the position is relative, not a fixed slice number. Attention can then use: [zi,pi] meaning: what is visible + where it occurs.

Training strategy Initially, DINOv2 should be frozen. Meaning: DINOv2 weights DO NOT update Train only: Module 1: position-aware attention classifier  Module 2: fusion MLP position-aware attention classifier This reduces: • GPU requirement,  • training time,  • overfitting risk.  It also allows DINO embeddings to be precomputed once. 
--- Page 19 ---
For example: all MRI slices      ↓ frozen DINOv2      ↓ save embeddings to disk Then the attention model trains on small vectors instead of repeatedly loading full MRI images. Later, perform an ablation: Frozen DINOv2 vs last 2–4 transformer blocks fine-tuned Full ViT fine-tuning is not the initial plan.  30. Data splitting This must be done patient-wise. (all slices of a patient in training must be in training only, must not be in testing )  Wrong: Patient A slice 1 → Train Patient A slice 2 → Train Patient A slice 18 → Test Correct: Patient A → Train Patient B → Train Patient C → Validation Patient D → Test All slices from one patient remain together. Explainability There are two different explanation levels. First: Which slices influenced the patient-level result? This comes from attention weights. For example: Slice 9  → 4% Slice 10 → 7% Slice 11 → 31% Slice 12 → 39% Slice 13 → 12% Second: Which region inside an important slice influenced DINOv2? A ViT-compatible attribution/attention-rollout method can generate a heatmap. Therefore the radiologist can potentially see: Patient classified Abnormal 
--- Page 22 ---
 Most influential slices: 11, 12, 13  Regional heatmap: highlighted suspicious region The heatmap is not a segmentation mask.  35. Research gaps we are trying to address A concise way to explain the methodological motivation is: Existing limitation Proposed response Some systems classify slices independently One patient-level classification Mean pooling treats every slice equally Learned attention weights Majority voting throws away feature information Aggregate rich DINO embeddings 2D models don't explicitly know slice location Relative slice-position encoding Fixed 3D CNNs often require fixed slice count Variable-length slice aggregation DWI-only stroke detection Explicit DWI–ADC fusion Many methods use conventional CNNs Investigate DINOv2 foundation features Tasks are studied separately Combine abnormality + stroke into triage Diagnostic output only Routine/Urgent/Immediate worklist priority Importantly, don't claim: “DINOv2 + attention has never been done.” DINOv2 slice aggregation already exists in the literature. Our stronger research framing is the combination of: general FLAIR abnormality + paired DWI–ADC stroke detection + variable-length patient-level aggregation + explicit slice position + final clinical triage fusion  36. Implementation sequence I would give the person The safest development order is: Stage 1: verify and organize the DICOM dataset. Stage 2: implement preprocessing and manually inspect outputs. Stage 3: get Module 1 working with frozen DINOv2 + simple mean pooling first. Stage 4: replace mean pooling with ordinary attention. Stage 5: add relative slice position → position-aware attention. Stage 6: develop Module 2 DWI/ADC pairing and simple fusion. Stage 7: add patient-level attention to Module 2. Stage 8: compare baselines and ablations. Stage 9: freeze both final modules and evaluate on integrated FLAIR+DWI+ADC patients. Stage 10: add decision fusion and explainability.
